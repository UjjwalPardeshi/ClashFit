The problem

Counting reps is
not coaching.

Every rep counter on a phone tells you that you moved. None of them tell you how — whether you reached depth, whether you controlled the descent, whether one side is quietly carrying the other, or whether the set you just did was actually hard for you today.

And every camera-based product that tries sends the video to a server. That is the most intimate sensor a phone has, pointed at you on your bedroom floor.

ClashFit measures all four, on the device, and uploads nothing.

What the phone measures

Four numbers,
not a verdict.

MediaPipe Pose reads 33 metric landmarks at 30 frames a second on the Snapdragon GPU. A hysteretic state machine over joint angles counts the rep; four geometric sub-scores grade it.

We never claim to measure “good form” — that is not a well-defined quantity. We measure four named things in degrees and centimetres, and we say exactly which one was worst.

A shallow rep does 50 damage. A clean one does 85. A perfect one does 100.

The boss cannot outlast you — but it makes you earn the ending. One fatigue model covers all five movement families: velocity for reps, tremor for holds, cadence for cardio, jump height for ballistics, accuracy drift for poses.

The signal a physio looks for

An average is
blind to this.

We compute the joint angle on both sides of your body, every frame. Most trackers average the two and throw the difference away.

That difference is the thing a physiotherapist actually looks at. Someone favouring one leg still produces a perfectly good average — they hit depth, tempo and range on the combined reading while loading one side noticeably less. That is the pattern that leads back to injury, and an averaged score cannot see it by construction.

Reported as a measured ratio with a confidence grade, never a diagnosis. A thin or one-sided view returns nothing rather than a number.

The second model

The coach and
the villain are
the same model.

After every set, eleven numbers describing how your movement changed go to Gemma 3n — a two-billion-parameter language model running on the phone's NPU. It writes the coaching. It also writes the boss.

Coach

“Your last three reps lost four centimetres of depth. Take forty seconds, then finish it.”

The Pacemaker

“Your knees are negotiating. I don't negotiate.”

Output is validated before it reaches the screen — two sentences, no invented numbers, nothing about your body. If the model is slow, missing or thermally throttled, a deterministic bank speaks instead and you cannot tell which fired.

Clinic

The tests a physio uses,
on a phone you own.

The 30-second sit-to-stand is a published functional assessment. On our side it is a timed chair squat on the detector that already ships.

Not a medical device. It does not diagnose and it does not recommend treatment. It runs the protocol, reports the count with its unit, and shows your own trend. The only recommendation it makes is to speak to a professional — and reference ranges ship deliberately empty until they can be properly cited.

Privacy

Zero bytes
leave the phone.

The camera is the most intimate sensor a phone has. Every other camera-based fitness product sends that video to a server.

Both models run on the device. There is no INTERNET permission in the release manifest — so the claim is checkable, not asserted. Open the manifest and look.
